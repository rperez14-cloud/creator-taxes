Behind on Taxes as a Creator? 4 IRS Relief Options Explained

Brand deals, AdSense and affiliate money can come in fast. Then tax time shows up and you owe more than you planned. The IRS has a few ways to help, but each one has limits. Here's how they work in plain English.

Why creators end up owing the IRS

When you work a W-2 job, your employer takes taxes out of every paycheck. As a creator, nobody does that for you. YouTube, TikTok and brands pay you the full amount, and the tax bill comes later.

Most creators who fall behind run into the same few problems.

The first one is skipping estimated payments. The IRS expects self-employed people to pay during the year, usually four times. If you wait until April, you may owe a penalty on top of the tax.

The second is income that jumps around. One month a brand deal pays $8,000. The next month you make $600. That makes it hard to guess what to send the IRS each quarter.

The third is self-employment tax. On top of income tax, you pay 15.3% for Social Security and Medicare. A regular employee splits that with their boss. You pay both halves.

Option 1: Ask the IRS to remove penalties

Penalties can grow your bill a lot. The IRS may remove some of them, but you usually have to qualify.

First-time penalty relief

If you filed and paid on time for the past three years, the IRS may remove a penalty for filing late or paying late. Starting with 2025 tax returns, the IRS has begun giving this relief automatically to people who qualify. For older years, you generally have to ask for it by phone or in writing.

Reasonable cause relief

If something serious kept you from filing or paying, like a major illness, a disaster or a death in the family, you can explain what happened and ask the IRS to remove the penalty. You'll need proof.

Heads up: First-time penalty relief does not cover the penalty for missing estimated tax payments. That penalty has its own narrow waiver rules, such as a disaster or other unusual event. You may see other articles that say the opposite.

Option 2: Set up an IRS payment plan

If you can't pay the full amount at once, a payment plan lets you pay over time. It does not lower what you owe, but it keeps you on good terms with the IRS.

Short-term plan

If you owe less than $100,000 and can pay it off within 180 days, you can ask for extra time with no setup fee.

Long-term plan

If you owe $50,000 or less and have filed all your returns, you can usually apply online and make monthly payments. A setup fee may apply.

Interest and some penalties keep adding up until the balance is paid. For late 2026, the IRS interest rate is 7% a year, and it can change every quarter. So it's smart to pay more than the minimum when a big check comes in.

Tip: File your return on time even if you can't pay. The penalty for filing late is usually much bigger than the penalty for paying late.

Option 3: Offer in Compromise

An Offer in Compromise lets some people settle their tax debt for less than they owe. It sounds great, and that's why you hear about it in so many ads.

The truth is that most people don't qualify. The IRS looks at your income, your bank accounts, what you own and your basic living costs. If it thinks you can pay the full amount over time, it will usually say no.

Watch out: Be careful with any company that promises to settle your debt for "pennies on the dollar." Nobody can promise that. Only the IRS decides.

If your debt is large, or you have years of unfiled returns, you may need an enrolled agent, CPA or tax attorney who handles IRS collections. If that's your situation, I'll tell you honestly and point you in the right direction.

Option 4: Lower next year's bill now

The best relief is the bill you never get. These steps help you avoid ending up here again.

  1. Set aside 25% to 30% of every payment in a separate savings account, and don't touch it.
  2. Make estimated payments. The next one for 2026 income is due January 15, 2027.
  3. If your income is uneven, ask about the IRS annualized method. It lets you pay less in slow quarters and more in busy ones, which can reduce or remove the underpayment penalty.
  4. Claim your write-offs. Equipment, editing software, part of your phone bill and a home studio may all lower your profit. See What Can Content Creators Write Off?
  5. Look at the self-employed retirement and health insurance deductions. They can lower your taxable income if you qualify.

What not to do

  • Don't ignore IRS letters. Things get worse and your options shrink.
  • Don't skip filing because you can't pay.
  • Don't spend your tax savings on a new camera.
  • Don't pay anyone who promises a specific settlement before looking at your numbers.

FAQ

Can content creators get IRS tax relief?

Yes. Creators can use the same IRS relief options as anyone else, as long as they meet the rules. The IRS looks at your income, what you own, and whether you filed your past returns.

What happens if I can't pay my taxes from YouTube or TikTok income?

File on time anyway, then ask for a payment plan. Ignoring the bill leads to more penalties and can lead to IRS collection action.

Can the IRS waive the penalty for missed estimated payments?

Sometimes, but it is harder to get than other penalty relief. First-time penalty relief does not cover it. The waiver is generally limited to disasters or other unusual events.

Do I need a company that advertises tax debt relief?

Not always. Many people can set up a payment plan on their own. For larger debts or collection notices, talk to a qualified tax professional before you pay anyone.
