What Can Content Creators Write Off? 12 Tax Deductions

If you make money from YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Twitch, or brand deals, you're running a small business. That means you can subtract many of your business costs before you pay tax. Here's what usually counts, what doesn't, and how to keep the IRS happy.

The one rule every write-off must pass ✅

A "write-off" (or deduction) is a business cost you subtract from your income. You pay tax on what's left, your profit, not on everything you earned.

To count, the IRS says a cost must be ordinary and necessary for your business:

  • Ordinary means it's common for creators. A ring light? Yes.
  • Necessary means it's helpful for making your content or earning money. It doesn't have to be required.

If you use something for both business and personal life, like your phone, you can only deduct the business part. And you always need proof of what you spent.

12 write-offs for content creators

1. 🎥 Cameras, mics, lights, and gear

Cameras, lenses, tripods, gimbals, microphones, ring lights, green screens, memory cards, and drones you use for content. Bigger items can often be deducted all in the year you buy them instead of spread out over several years. We'll pick the best option for your return.

2. 💻 Computer, phone, and tablet

If you edit on your laptop and film on your phone, those count. If you also use them for personal stuff, deduct the business share. Example: you use your phone 60% for content, so you deduct 60% of the cost.

3. 🧰 Software, apps, and subscriptions

Editing software (Adobe, Final Cut, CapCut Pro), Canva, music and sound effect libraries, stock footage, scheduling tools, cloud storage, and channel tools like TubeBuddy or vidIQ.

4. 🏠 Home office or studio

If part of your home is used regularly and only for your creator work, you may deduct it. A corner where you film and edit can count. Your living room couch that you also watch TV on does not.

There's a simple way to figure it: $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet (a max of $1,500). Or you can use a share of your real rent, utilities, and insurance, which can be bigger but takes more records.

5. 📶 Internet and phone bill

Uploading, livestreaming, and answering brand emails all use your internet and phone. Deduct the business share of your monthly bills.

6. 👥 Editors, designers, and your team

Money you pay to video editors, thumbnail designers, writers, virtual assistants, managers, and agents is deductible. Keep invoices, and ask each person for a Form W-9. Depending on how much you pay them, you may need to send them a Form 1099-NEC.

7. 🚗 Car and mileage

Driving to a shoot, a brand meeting, or to buy props counts. Driving from home to a regular job does not. Most creators use the IRS standard mileage rate. For 2026 it's 72.5 cents per mile from January to June and 76 cents per mile from July 1, after the IRS raised it mid-year.

Example: 1,000 business miles in the second half of 2026 = $760 deduction. You need a mileage log with the date, where you went, why, and how many miles. A free app works fine.

8. ✈️ Travel for content and events

Flights, hotels, and rides for a trip that's mainly for business, like filming on location, a creator conference, or a brand event. Business meals are usually 50% deductible. If you add vacation days, only the business part counts.

9. 🎭 Props, sets, and costumes

Backdrops, decorations for your set, props, and items you buy just to review or use in a video. Costumes and branded merch made for content count too. Normal clothes don't (more on that below).

10. 📣 Ads, website, and promotion

Paid ads, boosted posts, your website and domain, email marketing tools, link-in-bio tools, and business cards.

11. 📚 Courses and coaching

Courses, books, and coaching that help you get better at the work you already do, like a YouTube growth course, editing class, or a creator coach.

12. 💳 Fees the platforms take

Fees from Patreon, PayPal, Stripe, merch stores, and other platforms are business costs. Check that your income numbers and fee numbers line up, so you don't pay tax on money you never got.

💡 Quick tip: Gifted products you get in exchange for content are generally income at their fair market value. Track them along with your write-offs. We explain more on our FAQ.

3 bonus deductions most creators miss 🎁

These don't go on your business form (Schedule C). They go on your main Form 1040, but they can still lower your tax a lot.

  • Half of your self-employment tax. Creators pay about 15.3% self-employment tax for Social Security and Medicare. You get to deduct half of it.
  • Health insurance. If you pay for your own health insurance and you're not eligible for a plan through a job (yours or your spouse's), you can usually deduct the premiums.
  • Retirement savings. Accounts like a SEP IRA or Solo 401(k) let you save for the future and lower your taxes today.

What you usually can't write off 🚫

  • Everyday clothes, shoes, and makeup, even if you wear them on camera. If you could wear it out to dinner, it's personal.
  • Haircuts, nails, and gym memberships, in most cases. These are seen as personal, even for fitness or beauty creators.
  • Personal streaming services like your family Netflix plan.
  • Meals with friends, unless it's truly a business meeting.
  • IRS penalties, fines, and parking tickets.
  • Anything without proof. No record, no deduction.

⚠️ Be careful: "It's for content" doesn't make everything a write-off. Claiming personal costs can lead to an IRS letter, back taxes, and penalties. When in doubt, ask first.

How much can write-offs save you? 🧮

Here's a simple example. Maya is a single TikTok and YouTube creator.

Income from AdSense, brand deals, and affiliates$40,000
Write-offs (gear, software, editor, home office, mileage)− $12,000
Profit she pays tax on$28,000

That $12,000 in write-offs saves Maya about $1,700 in self-employment tax alone. On top of that, she pays less federal income tax, and often less state tax too. All in, she could keep $3,000 or more that would have gone to taxes.

Illustrative example. Your numbers depend on your income, filing status, and state.

How to keep records the easy way 🗂️

  1. Open a separate bank account and card just for your creator business. This one step makes everything easier.
  2. Save receipts right away. Snap a photo or forward the email to one folder.
  3. Track mileage as you drive with an app, not from memory in April.
  4. Write a quick note on bigger purchases: "Sony mic for podcast videos."
  5. Keep records at least 3 years after you file.
  6. Set aside money for taxes each month, and pay quarterly estimated taxes so April isn't a shock.

FAQ

Can I write off a camera I use for YouTube?

Yes. Cameras, microphones, lights, and lenses you use to make content are business expenses. If you also use the item for personal things, you can only deduct the business-use share. Keep the receipt.

Can influencers write off clothes and makeup?

Usually not. The IRS only allows clothing that is not suitable for everyday wear, like costumes or branded merch made for content. Normal clothes and everyday makeup are generally personal, even if you wear them on camera.

Do I need receipts for every write-off?

You need records that show what you paid, when, and why it was for your business. Receipts, invoices, bank statements, and a mileage log all count. Keep them for at least three years after you file.

Can I write off expenses if I don't make money yet?

Often yes, if you are running your channel like a real business with the goal of making a profit. The IRS looks at things like how you keep records and whether you are trying to earn money. If it looks like a hobby, the rules are much stricter.

Where do creators report their write-offs?

Most creators report income and business expenses on Schedule C, which is part of Form 1040. A few deductions, like self-employed health insurance and retirement contributions, go on Form 1040 instead.
